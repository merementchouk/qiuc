** Main principle

Adiabatic quantum computing (AQC) approaches solving problems similar to

\[
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^\star = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} H_{\mathrm{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
\]

through a gradual deformation of a simple physical system into one that represents the problem of interest. The word /adiabatic/ in *adiabatic* quantum computing refers to the /slow/ reshaping the problem.

AQC begins with representing the original classical problem, as a quantum one (a super-quick introduction to quantum Ising model is given below)

\[
H_{\mathrm{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m,n} A_{m,n} \sigma_m \sigma_n \to \widehat{H}_{\mathrm{P}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m,n} A_{m,n} \widehat{Z}_m \widehat{Z}_n.
\]


where \(\widehat{Z}_m\) are Pauli operators acting on the \(m\)-th qubit.

Next, an easy Hamiltonian (with \(h > 0\))

\[
\widehat{H}_0 = h \sum_{m} \widehat{X}_m
\]

is introduced, whose ground state is known and easy to prepare. Finally, this ground state evolves under a time-dependent Hamiltonian such as

\[
\widehat{H}(t) = [1-s(t)] \widehat{H}_0 + s(t)\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{P}},
\]

where \(T\) is the total evolution time, and \(s(t)\), a monotonous function taking values \(s(0)=0\) and \(s(T)=1\), is called an annealing schedule.

** The relationship between the quantum and classical formulations

Let's consider a quantum Hamiltonian

\[
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{P}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m,n} A_{m,n} \widehat{Z}_m \widehat{Z}_n.
\]

We notice a few things. First, the Pauli operators act according to \(\widehat{Z}_m \vert \sigma_m \rangle = \sigma_m \vert \sigma_m \rangle_m\), where \(\sigma_m = \pm 1\), and \(\vert \cdot \rangle_m\) denotes the state of the \(m\)-th qubit. In what follows, we will drop the index designating which qubit the state describes.

Next, the Hamiltonian $\widehat{H}_ P$ commutes with individual Pauli operators, \(\left[ \widehat{H}_ {\mathrm{P}}, \widehat{Z}_ m  \right] = 0\), and, therefore, the eignestates of \(\widehat{H}_ {\mathrm{P}}\) are states with definite \(\widehat{Z}_ m\). Thus, the eigenstates are product states, \(\vert \boldsymbol{\sigma} \rangle = \vert \sigma_1 \rangle \vert \sigma_2 \rangle  \ldots \vert \sigma_N \rangle\), with the eigenvalues

\[
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{P}} \vert \boldsymbol{\sigma} \rangle = H_{\mathrm{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \vert \boldsymbol{\sigma} \rangle.
\]

Thus, the ground state is \(\vert \boldsymbol{\sigma}^\star \rangle\), which is directly related to the binary spin configuration we are after.

In turn, the easy Hamiltonian \(\widehat{H}_0\) is constructed of Pauli operators that act according to \(\widehat{X}_m \vert \pm X \rangle = \pm \vert \pm X \rangle\), where \(\vert \pm X \rangle = \left( \vert + \rangle \pm \vert - \rangle \right) / \sqrt{2}\). Using the same argument as above, we find that the ground state of \(\widehat{H}_0\) is \(\vert - \mathbf{X} \rangle = \vert - X \rangle \vert - X \rangle \ldots \vert - X \rangle\).

** The adiabatic theorem and the instantaneous spectrum

The quantum adiabatic theorem provides the basic rationale for this procedure. In simplified terms, if the system starts in a nondegenerate (unique) ground state of \(H_0(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\), and if \(H(\boldsymbol{\sigma}; t)\) changes sufficiently slowly, then the evolving state remains close to the instantaneous ground state. At \(t=T\), measuring the system should therefore yield \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^\star\), a ground state of \(H_{\mathrm{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\), and hence a solution to the encoded problem.

Most of the excited states of the initial Hamiltonian are degenerate (question, which is not?). At \(t=0\), these states share the same energy and therefore appear as discrete points on the energy axis from which multiple spectral trajectories emerge. As the interpolation proceeds, the problem Hamiltonian generally breaks the relevant symmetries, lifting the degeneracy and separating these trajectories.

** The gap problem

The central practical issue is whether the instantaneous spectral gap \(
\Delta(t)=E_1(t)-E_0(t)\) between the ground state with the energy \(E_0(t)\), and the first excited state with the energy \(E_1(t)\),  remains sufficiently large. The most restrictive point is characterized by the minimum gap

\[
\Delta_{\min}=\min_{0\leq t\leq T}\Delta(t).
\]

Near a narrow avoided crossing, the instantaneous ground state can change rapidly. If the Hamiltonian is varied too quickly there, the system may undergo a transition into an excited state ([[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau%E2%80%93Zener_formula][the Landau-Zener effect]]). Quantitative adiabatic conditions depend on both the gap and matrix elements of \(\dot H(t)\); schematically, the required runtime often scales at least as an inverse power of \(\Delta_{\min}\). Consequently, an exponentially small gap may require an exponentially long evolution.

** Relevance to classical unconventional computing

For quantum-inspired unconventional computing, the transferable idea is broader than quantum coherence itself. A difficult configuration may be approached by continuously transforming a simple system with a known solution into a complex system encoding the target problem. The corresponding challenge is to choose the path, schedule, and dynamics so that the desired solution branch remains identifiable and stable throughout the transition. In a classical implementation, narrow quantum energy gaps may be replaced by other bottlenecks, such as bifurcations, metastability, or strong sensitivity to noise, but the underlying design problem remains much the same.

However, even if we don't have a universal solution for all possible problems (as we will see later, this is tightly related to the famous P vs NP problem), the adiabatic technique may be of great help for a particular class of them. So, it's useful to have it in the toolbox of approaches to test.
